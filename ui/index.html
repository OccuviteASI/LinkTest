About LinkTest

  
LinkTest  is a network toolbox for people who are not network engineers. Everything works offline, needs no installation and no administrator rights (except recording packets, which asks once).

  

  


  
Speed test

  
What it is for: measuring how fast two computers can move data between each other, which tells you what your cable, Wi‑Fi or switch really delivers.

  
    
    
  1. On one computer choose Be the test target and press Start. Note the address it shows.
    2. 
    
  2. On the other computer choose Test my connection, type that address, and press Start.
    3. 
    
  3. Read the verdict. Both computers are on your own network, so the number is your network's speed, not your internet provider's.
    4. 
  

  
Powered by iperf3 (BSD licence, github.com/esnet/iperf), bundled inside LinkTest.


  
Scan network

  
What it is for: finding out what is on your network. Press Scan and every device that answers is listed with its address, name, maker and the services (ports) it offers, such as a web page, remote desktop or file sharing. Use it to find a printer's address, spot unknown devices, or check that a device is reachable. From any row you can jump to Watch it or look up its name.


  
Ping monitor

  
What it is for: watching a connection over time to catch drop-outs and slow patches. Type an address and press Watch. The card shows the route hop by hop (like PingPlotter) with each router's reply time and loss, and below it the address's own reply-time chart. After three missed replies the card turns red and beeps; it turns back when replies return. History is kept for 30 days. Loss at a middle hop that does not continue to the hops after it is just that router being quiet, not a real problem.


  
DNS lookup

  
What it is for: checking how names turn into addresses. Type a name (or an address for the reverse) and see the records your DNS server returns, side by side with public servers such as Cloudflare and Google. Highlighted rows differ between servers, which usually points at a stale or wrong record.


  
Packet capture

  
What it is for: recording the actual traffic on a network connection when you need to see exactly what a device is doing, the way Wireshark does. Choose the connection, what to record and how long, then press Start. The viewer explains each packet in plain language, lets you filter by type, address or a word, and you can hand the file to Wireshark for the full detail. On Windows the built-in Npcap engine is used when Wireshark or Npcap is installed; otherwise Windows asks for administrator permission once per recording.

  
Connections & findings (under the packet list) reads the capture the way the Zeek network monitor does: it groups packets into conversations and writes one line per connection, web request, name lookup, secure connection, certificate, file, SSH login, DHCP lease and so on, in Zeek's own log format. "What stands out" lists problems in plain language: connections that keep failing, heavy retransmission, DNS servers not answering, expired or self-signed certificates, old encryption, passwords sent in the clear, rogue DHCP servers, IP conflicts and scans. Click a line to see every field; "Everything about this connection" follows one conversation across all the logs, and "Show its packets" jumps to them in the packet list. "Save Zeek logs" writes conn.log, dns.log, http.log… that Zeek tools, Splunk and Elastic read directly. It also works for any .pcap or .pcapng from Wireshark (Open a capture file…). From a terminal: LinkTest --zeek-logs capture.pcapng writes the logs next to the file.


  
Words you may see

  

    
Mbps / Gbps
Megabits or gigabits per second. Divide by 8 for megabytes per second, the number file copies show.

    
Retransmits
Pieces of data that had to be sent twice. A handful is normal; thousands mean the link is losing data.

    
Jitter
How uneven the packet timing is. Under 30 ms is fine for calls.

    
Packet loss
Share of packets that never arrived. Under 1% is fine for calls; a speed test should show almost none.

    
Hop
A router your traffic passes through on the way to its destination.

    
Port
A numbered door on a device; each service listens on its own (80/443 web, 22 SSH, 3389 Remote Desktop, 445 Windows sharing).

    
TCP / UDP
The two ways data travels: TCP checks everything arrived (web pages, files), UDP is quick and unchecked (calls, video, DNS).

    
Packet
One small chunk of data on the wire. A web page is thousands of them.
